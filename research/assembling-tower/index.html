The ninety-two days
nobody can see.
Until now.

Ninety-two days is your own average to fully settle a house claim. Most of those days are honest work — assessors, schedules, the Natural Hazards portion — but your customer sees none of it. This is a concept for making the wait visible, valuable, and shorter-feeling: the claim prepared in the open, and every decision still made by a person.

01 — THE WAIT
A Wednesday, 7:12am

The morning after the storm

Water came through the ceiling overnight. Lodging the claim in My Tower takes minutes — your own site says online claims save an average of five minutes, and photos help validate twice as fast. Then the part nobody designed: the silence between lodgement and a decision, measured in weeks, while the customer refreshes an app that has nothing new to say.

The 2023 floods and Cyclone Gabrielle brought Tower 8,900 claims — your chief claims officer called it five years of large-loss claims in about two weeks. Half-year profit to March 2026 more than halved on $18.5m of large-event costs, while 15,000 new customers arrived in a year. The next event is a when. The claims experience during it is a choice.

Live — the wait itself, not a picture of it. It stops to ask you one thing.

What happens now

The claim is real work from the first hour — triage, photos, the assessor diary, the Natural Hazards portion. All of it invisible, so every day of it reads as neglect.

What it costs

In the 2023 surge, 35% of enquiries reaching the insurance ombudsman were about delays, against a 9% norm. The work was being done. Nobody could see it.

What the concept does

Shows the claim being prepared as it happens, pays the wait back as excess credit, asks the one question that changes triage — and stops. A person decides.

This is not a new idea — it is an unbuilt one.

US retailers lose an estimated $37.7 billion a year to customers who abandon because of long queues, split roughly $15.8b to competitors and $21.9b abandoned outright (Adyen with 451 Research). The New York State Department of Labor reported a 48% reduction in hang-ups after introducing virtual queuing. Theme parks moving to virtual queues reported a 36% lift in per-capita spending, because guests spent the wait somewhere that sold something. A Seattle-Tacoma Airport study found 63% of passengers said they shopped or ate more because they saved time in the security queue.

Every figure on this page carries its source. Overseas figures are shown as overseas figures — we have not found a published New Zealand equivalent, and we will not invent one.

Layer 1 · The interface

The claim, rendered

A live panel showing this property against Tower’s own published flood-risk view, the assessment progressing, and which portion is Natural Hazards Commission rather than Tower. The customer stops guessing which queue they are in.

Layer 2 · The co-pilot

A labelled line, not a chatbot

It answers what stage the claim is at and what happens next, from the claim record rather than a script. It says when it does not know and hands to a person. Every answer is marked as prepared by a machine.

Layer 3 · The commerce

The wait pays for itself

While the assessment completes, one funded slot: temporary accommodation, drying and make-safe, or replacement essentials. It is relevant because the claim says the house is wet — not because someone bid highest.

Layer 4 · The exchange

One question, answered once

Inside the wait: is the house safe to sleep in tonight? An answer to that changes the triage immediately, and it is the one question a survey three weeks later never gets.

09 — WHAT WE ARE ACTUALLY ASKING
The boundary

What this will never do

It does not send. It prepares, and a named person sends. It does not publish to any channel, commit spend, move money, or make a decision that belongs to a person. It does not pretend to be a person — every draft says it was prepared by a machine and approved by a human.

No production access is requested by this concept.

The pilot ask
1

Scope

One weather event’s house-claim cohort, lodgement to decision-drafted. Six weeks. Not the whole claims book, not assessment itself, not settlement.

2

Access

Policy wording, your claim-status field names, your update templates, and the address risk-rating band. No customer records. No claims history. No settlement figures.

3

Scorecard

Updates accepted without rewrite. Held files a person agreed should have been held. Status calls the contact centre no longer received. One claims manager’s honest answer to “would you send this?”

Fail any line of the scorecard and we change the design or stop.
