# Agentic AI in retirement living — research for Summerset and Ryman concepts

**Compiled** 30 July 2026 · assembl · for Kate Hudson
**Status** research only. Neither Summerset Group Holdings Limited nor Ryman Healthcare Limited has commissioned, reviewed, endorsed or been approached about anything in this document. Every concept in it is unsolicited and speculative.

## How to read the labels

Every claim carries one of three labels.

- **[PUBLISHED]** — the organisation's own material: its website, its statutory disclosure statement, its annual report, its newsroom.
- **[REPORTED]** — press, trade press or analyst. Second-hand.
- **[INFERENCE]** — mine. Not sourced, and marked so you never quote it as fact.

Where I could not source something, it is in *What I could not verify* at the end rather than softened into the body.

## Two corrections to our existing notes

Before anything else, because both were live in our own material.

**1. Ryman's standard deferred management fee is 30%, not 20%.** Ryman's own published article states: "The DMF is calculated as a percentage of the entry price and Ryman's standard DMF is calculated at 30%." **[PUBLISHED]** — [rymanhealthcare.co.nz/what-we-offer/useful-articles/deferred-management-fee](https://www.rymanhealthcare.co.nz/what-we-offer/useful-articles/deferred-management-fee), Ryman Healthcare. The same 30% figure appears on Ryman's Financial Certainty page — [rymanhealthcare.co.nz/financial-certainty](https://www.rymanhealthcare.co.nz/financial-certainty) — and on its Australian site — [rymanhealthcare.com.au/planning-retirement/useful-articles/deferred-management-fee](https://www.rymanhealthcare.com.au/planning-retirement/useful-articles/deferred-management-fee).

The 20% figure was correct historically. RNZ reported on 2 September 2024 that Ryman was moving from 20% to "25 or 30 percent, depending on the initial entry price paid" **[REPORTED]** — [rnz.co.nz/news/business/526811](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/business/526811/ryman-healthcare-raises-prices-to-fill-financial-holes), RNZ. Ryman told The Weekly Source: "There will never be changes to the DMFs or fixed for life weekly fees of our current Ryman residents." **[REPORTED]** — [theweeklysource.com.au](https://www.theweeklysource.com.au/under-pressure-ryman-healthcare-reviewing-its-20-dmf-set-for-life-weekly-fees-and-developments/), The Weekly Source, 6 August 2024.

So there are two populations of Ryman residents on different terms. Any concept that quotes a DMF must ask which cohort the family is in, or it will be wrong for half of them.

**2. Summerset *does* publish a link between its weekly fee and NZ Superannuation.** Our note said it does not. That is true of summerset.co.nz. It is **not** true of Summerset's statutory disclosure statement, which is a public document filed on the Retirement Villages Register. The Summerset on the Landing disclosure statement, disclosure date 1 January 2026, states verbatim:

> "The Weekly Fee will increase annually on 1 July each year. The annual increase will be the same as the percentage increase in the after-tax New Zealand Superannuation Rate, based on the percentage increase for the previous 12-month period, 1 April to 31 March and will be advised to you before 1 June each year."

and, in the summary section:

> "This fee may increase annually, however, any percentage increase in this fee will never exceed the percentage increase in the after tax rate of New Zealand Superannuation in that year."

**[PUBLISHED]** — Disclosure Statement, Summerset on the Landing, disclosure date 1 January 2026, operator Summerset Villages (Kenepuru) Limited, filed on the Retirement Villages Register, New Zealand Companies Office — [srp.companiesoffice.govt.nz](https://srp.companiesoffice.govt.nz/_entity/annotation/ee45c57e-6ddc-f011-8544-002248921145/f3ede290-2de6-413d-89c9-2bf1566d117f?t=1780790400221).

This correction matters more than the fact itself, and it is the single most useful thing I found. **The answers families actually want are published — in 42-page statutory PDFs on a Companies Office register, not on the operator's website.** That gap is the whole opportunity, and it is defensible ground because nothing has to be invented to fill it.

One caution: the disclosure statement is per village and per operator entity. The NZ Super clause is verified for Summerset on the Landing (Kenepuru) as at 1 January 2026. I did not check all 38 villages. Treat it as village-specific until confirmed across the estate.

## What is actually shipping worldwide

The short version: **a great deal is installed, almost nothing is agentic, and operators publish far less than vendors do.**

### The operators are nearly silent, and that is the finding

- **Brookdale Senior Living's FY2025 10-K mentions AI only as a risk.** Verbatim: competitors "may incorporate advanced technology, including artificial intelligence, into their business operations or services more quickly or more successfully than us"; and "To the extent we integrate AI into our operations, this may increase the cybersecurity and privacy risks". Note "to the extent" — no deployment is asserted. A text search of the full filing returns zero hits for `SafelyYou`, `fall detection`, `predictive`, `algorithm`, `machine learning`, `wearable` and `Alexa`. Brookdale HealthPlus is described as "community-based, technology-enabled, proactive care coordination", explicitly not AI. **[PUBLISHED]** — [sec.gov, Brookdale FY2025 10-K, 19 Feb 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1332349/000133234926000032/bkd-20251231.htm). Board-level AI governance does exist: the Audit Committee "regularly reviews … risks, policies, and governance associated with artificial intelligence (AI)" **[PUBLISHED]** — [DEF 14A, 30 Apr 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1332349/000119312526197412/d949610ddef14a.htm).
- **Sonida Senior Living is the only US operator I could find asserting AI deployment in its own filing.** Verbatim: "We are integrating generative AI tools into certain of our systems"; and "Recent developments in AI, such as generative or agentic AI, may accelerate or exacerbate these effects". No product named, no outcome. **[PUBLISHED]** — [Sonida FY2025 10-K, 12 Mar 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1043000/000104300026000009/snda-20251231.htm).
- **The disclosure gap, quantified.** Sonida has 26 communities live on SafelyYou per the vendor's own release, yet its FY2025 10-K returns zero hits for `SafelyYou`, `fall detection`, `ambient` and `wearable`. **[PUBLISHED]** — both sources above. **[INFERENCE]** Operators do not treat deployed AI as a disclosable strategic asset. That is the commercial opening: nobody has claimed this ground publicly.
- **The one audited proxy for real installed base came from a landlord, not an operator.** Omega Healthcare Investors: "In the third quarter of 2021, we made an investment of $20.0 million in SafelyYou, Inc. … we obtained preferred shares representing 5% of the outstanding equity … As of December 31, 2023, 30% of the SafelyYou warrants have vested as a result of certain installation targets being met." **[PUBLISHED]** — [Omega FY2023 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/888491/000088849124000007/ohi-20231231x10k.htm). `SafelyYou` returns zero hits in Omega's [FY2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/888491/000088849125000006/ohi-20241231x10k.htm) and [FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/888491/000088849126000008/ohi-20251231x10k.htm) filings. Why is not stated and I am not going to guess.
- **Genuine operator-published deployments do exist, and they are the models to imitate.** Leisure Care publishes its SafelyYou rollout community by community inside its Opal Memory Care programme — "AI-enabled devices in private memory care apartment homes to detect falls the moment they happen" **[PUBLISHED]** — [leisurecare.com](https://www.leisurecare.com/resources/safely-you-fall-detection/). ONELIFE Senior Living publishes its own with an unusually honest disclaimer worth copying verbatim: "Outcome statistics are based on external studies and multi-community implementations, not guaranteed for any single resident or community. Results vary." **[PUBLISHED]** — [onelifeseniorliving.com, 17 Oct 2025](https://www.onelifeseniorliving.com/blog/safelyyou-at-onelife-fewer-falls-more-peace-of-mind).
- **Voice, at scale, with an engagement number.** Amazon's Alexa Smart Properties for Senior Living names Atria Engage Life, Atlas and Fellowship Square-Mesa, and publishes "increased participation in Atria Engage Life® events by 50-75%". **[PUBLISHED]** — [developer.amazon.com](https://developer.amazon.com/en-US/alexa/alexasmartproperties/seniorliving). Note this is Amazon publishing, not Atria.

### Agentic AI, specifically, is barely real

- The only product I found marketed as agentic in senior living is **Olia** from iN2L + LifeLoop — "a fully agentic AI modelled after the very best Life Enrichment Directors". Status verbatim: "Olia is coming soon, and we want to refine it with you", with an Early Adopter Program and six named design partners: DIAL Senior Living, Merrill Gardens, New Perspective, The Bristal Assisted Living, Sinceri Senior Living, Phoenix Senior Living. No outcome statistics. **[PUBLISHED]** — [lifeloop.com/olia](https://www.lifeloop.com/olia).
- The nearest thing to a shipped agent in aged care is Sensi.AI, which "is creating an AI agent to handle initial client inquiries and scheduling coordination" — i.e. still being built. **[REPORTED]** — [PR Newswire via Yahoo Finance, 4 Jun 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/healthcare/articles/home-halo-partners-sensi-ai-140000086.html).

**[INFERENCE]** This is the single best strategic fact in the file. In July 2026, the words "agentic" and "retirement village" have almost no shipped intersection anywhere in the world. Neither Summerset nor Ryman is behind — the category is empty.

### Adoption is genuinely low

PointClickCare's own survey of decision-makers: "79% of decision-makers are optimistic or excited about AI's potential", but "**only 10% currently use AI in their operations**" and "65% of participants report complete unfamiliarity with AI". **[PUBLISHED]** — [pointclickcare.com, 2 Jun 2026](https://pointclickcare.com/press-releases/pointclickcare-launches-advisor-suite-expanding-ai-native-workflow-automation-solutions-for-skilled-nursing/). Sample size not published.

### New Zealand: Summerset and Ryman are not the leaders here

This is the competitive picture, and it should change the urgency of the pitch.

- **Bupa New Zealand has the most concrete deployment in the country.** Verbatim: "Bupa New Zealand has rolled out **Nexus AI**, an AI-supported care platform designed to help frontline teams deliver safer and more responsive care to residents." Sandy Turnwald, Clinical and Quality Director: "With Nexus AI, our people can simply ask a question and quickly access the relevant current information", and "Healthcare is human. It requires compassion, connection and professional judgement." Published boundary worth borrowing: the platform "supports clinical, operational, and care teams **without automating care itself**". Vendor not named. **[PUBLISHED]** — [bupa.co.nz, 2 Jun 2026](https://www.bupa.co.nz/life-at-bupa/news/bupa-invests-in-ai-to-support-smarter-safer-aged-care/).
  Bupa also claims a world first: a digital Deterioration Early Warning System in VCare — "becoming the first aged care provider in New Zealand to implement the technology and delivering a world-first for digitally integrated early deterioration detection in residential aged care", with "The rollout will benefit all 3000 Bupa residents across 40 sites". Built on the paper-based DEWS from Te Tāhū Hauora Health Quality & Safety Commission and the University of Auckland. Pilot outcomes: "reductions in falls, behaviours, pressure injuries and restraint use, with Bupa Northhaven becoming restraint-free." **[PUBLISHED]** — [bupa.co.nz, 12 Mar 2026](https://www.bupa.co.nz/life-at-bupa/news/world-first-digital-deterioration-early-warning-system-in-aged-care/). And it ships a family-facing portal, **blua**, "currently available by invitation only at three care home sites" **[PUBLISHED]** — [bupa.co.nz/blua](https://www.bupa.co.nz/blua/).
- **Metlifecare has the most structured plan.** Verbatim: "We have established foundational artificial intelligence (AI) governance and developed a **four-pillar AI transformation approach**. This encompasses data foundations enabling predictive insights, AI assistants accelerating operational efficiency, clinical AI enhancing personalised resident care, and business function modernisation delivering enterprise-wide productivity." Also: "Artificial Intelligence (AI) was also a key area of focus for the Board during FY25", and "A new digital resident portal will be piloted in mid-FY26". CIO Tim Aynsley, joined August 2024. **[PUBLISHED]** — [Metlifecare Annual Report 2025](https://cdn.sanity.io/files/hss7u6go/production/6b6c731b52de4e2478f19c2318e527b5a62646ce.pdf).
- **Oceania mentions AI only as a cyber threat** — "Strengthened cyber strategy to address emerging threats, including AI enabled cyber attacks." Resident tech is the non-AI Oceania Together App. Its FY26 Investor Presentation contains no AI mention at all. **[PUBLISHED]** — [FY25 Annual Integrated Report](https://api.oceaniahealthcare.co.nz/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/FY25-Annual-Intergrated-Report.pdf).
- **Arvida has published nothing.** Its FY2026 Annual Report, extracted in full at 14,225 words, contains zero occurrences of "artificial intelligence", "AI" or "digital" beyond accounting-policy lines on software amortisation. **[PUBLISHED]** — [FY2026 Annual Report](https://www.arvida.co.nz/web-assets/images/Import/20.investors_3169/07.results-reports_3310/FY2026-Arvida-Annual-Report.pdf).

**[INFERENCE]** So the NZ field has Bupa shipping care AI plus an invitation-only family portal, Metlifecare with board-level AI governance and a resident portal piloting mid-FY26, Summerset piloting two AI tools it discusses only with investors, Ryman with AI in strategy and no product, and Oceania and Arvida absent. **Nobody has aimed any of it at the buying family.** That remains open — but the window is narrower than it looks, because Metlifecare's resident portal pilot and Bupa's blua are both moving toward that space from the resident side.

### Australia: two operator deployments worth studying, one of them on Claude

- **ECH (South Australia) is the best-documented named-operator deployment I found in Australasia, and it runs on Claude.** AWS Transcribe for audio wellbeing interviews, then AWS Bedrock with **Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2** producing structured care plans aligned to ECH standards. Figures quoted by the implementation partner: care plan creation "from 20 minutes to just 36 seconds"; accuracy "from 35.3% to 92.5%"; consistency "exceeded 85%"; "$138,060 per year" and "3,068 hours" saved across 2,300 clients. **[PUBLISHED]** by vendor CGI — [cgi.com](https://www.cgi.com/au/en-au/case-study/health/improving-quality-aged-care-generative-ai). **[INFERENCE]** This is the single most useful precedent in the file for us specifically: a named Australasian aged-care provider, hard before/after numbers, and Claude in production. It answers "has anyone actually done this in our part of the world" with a yes.
- **Regis Healthcare is the only Australian operator with a self-published, human-in-the-loop AI deployment.** Its FY25 Annual Report carries a dedicated heading, "AI in Action: Building Scalable, Smarter Aged Care": AI analyses daily progress notes for Clinical Care Managers, detecting pain, infection and behavioural changes; analysis that took hours completes "in minutes"; recommendations are grounded in Regis's internal clinical policies and **reviewed by a Clinical Care Manager** — human-in-the-loop is explicit. Named pilot sites: Regis Birkdale and Regis Dandenong North. **[PUBLISHED]** — [Regis FY25 Annual Report](https://www.regis.com.au/site/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Regis-Healthcare-Limited-FY25-Annual-Report.pdf). The tool is named **RegiCare Assist**, built on Microsoft Copilot Studio and Microsoft Foundry, rolled out "September 2025", "150 staff daily", where managers previously read "up to 80 pages of progress notes"; CIO Imtiaz Bhayat. **[REPORTED]** — [news.microsoft.com](https://news.microsoft.com/signal/articles/regis-cio-shares-his-tips-on-bringing-people-along-on-the-ai-journey/).
  **[INFERENCE]** Note the pattern, because it repeats: Regis's AI disclosure exists **only** in the annual report. All 40 items in its newsroom were checked and none mentions AI or RegiCare Assist. Summerset does exactly the same thing. Operators in this sector tell investors about AI and tell customers nothing.
- **Aveo, Levande, Opal HealthCare and Keyton have published nothing on AI in care.** Keyton's only AI reference is website personalisation in its cookie notice. Australian Unity's published AI is in **waste management** — "operational cost savings of up to 20 percent" — not care. **[PUBLISHED]** — operator sites and annual reports as checked.

### The UK: eight for eight, nothing on AI

Not one of Anchor, McCarthy Stone, Audley, Retirement Villages Group, Inspired Villages, Riverstone, Legal & General/Guild Living or Birchgrove has published anything about AI. Two full-text greps confirm it: Anchor's Annual Report & Financial Statements 2025 (404,914 characters extracted) and Audley Group's ESG Report 2024 (110,509 characters) each return **zero** matches for `artificial intelligence`, `machine learning`, `AI`, `chatbot`, `algorithm` and `predictive analytic`. **[PUBLISHED]** — [Anchor](https://anchorv3dev.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/documents-pdfs/within-investors-section/Anchor_Annual_Report_%26_Financial_Statements_2025_FINAL_Compressed.pdf), [Audley](https://audleygroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Audley-Group-ESG-Report-2024.pdf).

What they publish instead is back-office digitisation. Anchor is the most advanced — a 10-year Plentific repairs partnership as "the central hub for Anchor's 200,000 work orders per year" across "65,000 residents across 54,000 homes", and Wifinity across "almost 1,700 housing locations and care homes" **[PUBLISHED]** — [anchor.org.uk](https://www.anchor.org.uk/media/press-room/anchor-pioneers-digital-era-property-operations-plentific).

**The one exception is worth knowing.** **Birchgrove** is the only later-living operator in the set with a published resident *and family* app: "The Birchgrove App is invite-only and exclusively accessible to residents of Birchgrove neighbourhoods **and their families**" **[PUBLISHED]** — [birchgrove.life/app](https://www.birchgrove.life/app). It is also the only one with a named data executive, "Matt Steere, Data Director". No AI claim anywhere.

**And the UK's best-evidenced AI-in-care result has a public payer behind it.** Cera's fall prediction, published by NHS England, 5 March 2025: "predict a patient's risk of falling with 97% accuracy"; "2 million patient home care visits a month"; "more than two thirds of NHS integrated care systems"; "5,000 high-risk alerts a day"; "reducing hospitalisations by up to 70%"; falls cost "£2 billion"; "saving the NHS over £1 million a day". **[PUBLISHED]** — [england.nhs.uk](https://www.england.nhs.uk/2025/03/nationwide-roll-out-of-artificial-intelligence-tool-that-predicts-falls-and-viruses/).

**Handle that 97% carefully.** On Cera's own site the framing is different: "we can pinpoint **97% of falls**, and predict falls in the next 7 days with **83% accuracy**" **[PUBLISHED]** — [ceracare.co.uk](https://ceracare.co.uk/home-care-local-authority-page). So 97% is a detection figure for Cera and an accuracy figure for NHS England. If the number is used, say which framing — and note that **no independent or peer-reviewed validation of Cera's model was found**; NHS England repeats the figures rather than validating them.

**PainChek is the best-evidenced AI actually inside care homes**, and it connects directly to Summerset. Described by DHSC as "an AI-driven pain assessment app": "1,400+ residents supported", "465 individuals in initial pilot", "14,200+ assessments completed" as of mid-May 2025, "764 residents across 25 care locations" in Bedfordshire, Luton and Milton Keynes. **[PUBLISHED]** — [digitisingsocialcare.co.uk, 24 Jun 2025](https://beta.digitisingsocialcare.co.uk/news/facial-analysis-app-helps-reduce-pain). **[INFERENCE]** Given Summerset's Australian privacy policy discloses AI "tools that assist our staff to assess pain levels experienced by our residents", PainChek is the obvious candidate — but Summerset does not name a vendor and I have not confirmed it. Do not assert the link.

## The family's journey

### The buyer is the adult child — and here is the only published proof I could find

Roobrik publishes who actually completes its decision surveys. Verbatim:

- Driving Safety survey: "Typical usage: **30% self, 70% family members/other**"
- Cognitive Changes survey: "Typical usage: **40% self, 60% family members/other**"

**[PUBLISHED]** — [roobrik.com/senior-living-surveys](https://roobrik.com/senior-living-surveys/). Treat as tool-specific usage data, not a market-wide enquiry share — but it is the best published evidence that the researcher is not the prospective resident.

Roobrik Afford is built on that premise explicitly: "a conversational, non-invasive approach to affordability, so **adult children and other decision-makers can assess financial readiness without needing access to sensitive details**." And: "Simplify the financial evaluation process without asking for a single number." **[PUBLISHED]** — [roobrik.com/afford-senior-living-survey](https://roobrik.com/afford-senior-living-survey/).

### Summerset already collects the adult-child signal and does nothing with it

Summerset's information pack form asks whether the enquirer is "a potential resident" or "**looking on behalf of someone else**". **[PUBLISHED]** — [summerset.co.nz/contact-us/get-an-information-pack](https://www.summerset.co.nz/contact-us/get-an-information-pack/).

**[INFERENCE]** The single highest-value, lowest-cost move available to Summerset. The segmentation question is already asked and answered by the family, and the journey downstream of it is identical either way. Branching on that one field is a day of work and it is the entire premise of the family concepts.

### The decision has two shapes, not one

Aline's 2026 benchmark: "**Move-ins remain heavily concentrated within 30 days**"; inquiries "declined year over year"; Independent Living showed "double-digit growth in inquiries and improved conversion performance"; Assisted Living had "the sharpest drop in demand"; "more than 95,000 active researchers in 2025"; "engagement peaking between 3 and 6 p.m." **[PUBLISHED]** — [alineops.com](https://alineops.com/senior-living-sales-marketing-benchmark-report-2026/). Full report gated.

"It takes 20 to 50 touchpoints to move a prospect from initial contact to move-in" **[REPORTED]** — Aline 2024 Benchmark Report as cited in Aline's own blog. Digital leads are "making up to 60% of all leads" **[PUBLISHED]** — [alineops.com press release](https://alineops.com/press-releases/aline-acquires-roobrik/).

**[INFERENCE]** A 30-day concentration alongside a 20–50 touchpoint journey means two populations: a slow, years-long "thinking about it" cohort and a fast, crisis-driven cohort acting inside a month. They need opposite treatment. The slow cohort needs patience and no contact; the fast cohort needs answers in one sitting. A single funnel serves neither, and pressuring the fast cohort — who are frightened — is where this category does its real harm.

Claims "+186%" leads and "+87%" move-ins. A Place for Mom is paid by our senior living network communities when a family we refer chooses to move into a community." **[PUBLISHED]** — same source. Lottie repeats "free" without disclosing who pays.

**No senior living operator anywhere has shipped an LLM assistant on its own sales site.** Every AI product found is a vendor layer sold to operators. **[INFERENCE]**, from an exhaustive negative search.

**[INFERENCE]** New Zealand has the highest retirement village density in the world and not one family-facing tool — public or private — that answers what it costs. That is the gap, stated as plainly as it can be.

## The questions nobody answers well

### "What will it really cost over time?"

Nobody publishes a multi-year total-cost model for a New Zealand family. The components are all published; nothing assembles them.

What *is* published, and this is the useful part — **Summerset's expected tenure by unit type**, which is the missing multiplier for any honest total-cost figure. Verbatim: tenure is "estimated to be seven to eight years for villas, five to six years for apartments, three years for serviced apartments and memory care apartments, and two years for care suites". Also: "The vast majority of our resale homes sell within four to five months." **[PUBLISHED]** — [Summerset Annual Report 2025](https://assets.ctfassets.net/hjeh5mvg3dxz/2jnhMVUuNIqYJjtLrjEUYY/a8d205014126db0536258770f8e75cee/Summerset_Annual_Report_2025.pdf).

**[INFERENCE]** Put tenure next to the DMF accrual schedule and the honest answer appears: a villa resident whose expected tenure is seven to eight years will, in nearly every case, pay the **full** 25% — because accrual completes in year four or five. The "25% over four years" framing invites families to imagine a partial fee. For the average villa resident it is not partial. Summerset publishes both halves of this and never puts them together. Doing so is the most valuable and most uncomfortable thing any concept here could do.

Third-party attempts, for completeness: villageguide.co.nz publishes "Most retirement villages have a Deferred Management Fee of between 25% and 30%" with a worked $1,000,000 villa example; Eldernet publishes a DMF example accruing "at 5% per year, and … capped at a total of 20%". Both **[PUBLISHED]** but with the publisher-disclosure problems noted above, and neither matches Summerset's or Ryman's actual terms.

### "What happens when Mum's care needs change?"

The worst-served question of the five, and the highest-stakes.

Summerset publishes the promise — residents "can move from an independent home or apartment into care within the same village" — with the material caveat "(subject to availability and eligibility criteria)". **[PUBLISHED]** — Annual Report 2025. A full-text search of that report found **no disclosure of whether a further DMF applies on transfer into a serviced apartment or care.**

The answer exists — but only in the statutory disclosure statement, which is where I found the 25% overall cap, the carry-across of accrued percentage, the 2% transfer administration fee and its waivers, and the Dual Occupancy Fee capped at 10%. See *Verified: Summerset*. **[INFERENCE]** A family reading the annual report and the website cannot answer this question. A family reading the disclosure statement can. That is a document-legibility problem, not an information problem, and it is solvable without inventing anything.

Ryman answers part of it well and publishes it plainly: "Your DMF will not increase - even if you transfer to another townhouse or apartment within the village, or if you transfer to another Ryman village"; "No additional capital is required if you transfer from independent living to a standard serviced apartment"; "there are no transfer fees". **[PUBLISHED]** — [rymanhealthcare.co.nz](https://www.rymanhealthcare.co.nz/what-we-offer/useful-articles/deferred-management-fee). This is genuinely better than Summerset's public position on transfers, and Ryman should be told so.

The only NZ resources aimed squarely at this question are the Retirement Commission's 2020 "Transition to Care Disclosure Guidelines" and its 2018 "Care pathways" diagram. **[PUBLISHED]** — as cited above.

### "What comes back at the end?"

Best-served of the five, because reform has forced it into the open. See *NZ regulation and sensitivity* for the statutory position.

Consumer NZ has pushed hardest. Verbatim, from "Top 10 fixes for retirement villages", 18 April 2024, Rebecca Styles: "the capital sum, less fees, should be paid to the outgoing resident within 28 days of leaving"; "Operators should stop charging any fees when a resident has left their unit. Yet some continue charging until the unit is reoccupied."; and the clearest illustration of capital loss anyone has published — "If you paid $700,000 for the right to occupy your unit, but 5 years later it sells for $850,000, the village usually gets to keep the extra $150,000." **[PUBLISHED]** — Consumer NZ.

Summerset states its practice already exceeds the incoming rules, verbatim: "our weekly fees stop as soon as a resident leaves the village and we already pay interest to a resident, or their estate, if their home is unsold after six months. Though not a contractual obligation we typically also repurchase in the rare cases where a home hasn't sold within 12 months." **[PUBLISHED]** — Annual Report 2025. **[INFERENCE]** That is a strong, checkable claim that appears in an investor document and nowhere a family will see it. Moving it to the family surfaces costs nothing and is worth more than any campaign.

### "Will Mum actually be happy there?"

**There is a published New Zealand answer to this and families are never shown it.**

**[INFERENCE]** Summerset has a measured, executive-incentivised, three-times-a-year answer to the most emotional question in the category, published only for investors. Surfacing it — with the methodology and the trend, not just the headline — is the strongest single move available to either brand, and it is available to Summerset today.

Least-served of the five in New Zealand. ## Care-side AI: real, and regulated

### The whole category is unregulated, and that is the headline

An FDA 510(k) applicant-name search for **SafelyYou, VirtuSense, CarePredict, Nobi, Vayyar, Intuition Robotics and Palarum** returned **zero clearances for every one**. None of their own sites claims FDA clearance. **[PUBLISHED]** — [openFDA 510(k) database](https://api.fda.gov/device/510k.json?search=applicant:%22SafelyYou%22), queried 30 July 2026. Caveat: an applicant-name search can miss subsidiaries and exempt pathways, so read this as "no clearance found under these names", not as absolute.

**[INFERENCE]** So everything below — including products that predict falls and forecast health deterioration — is sold as wellness or operational technology, not as a regulated medical device. In New Zealand the binding constraints would instead be the Health Information Privacy Code 2020, the Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025 where cameras or gait are involved, and the Ngā Paerewa Health and Disability Services Standard through the certification and audit regime. The absence of device regulation is not an absence of regulation, and a CTO will know that.

### The one piece of real evidence

Everything else in this section is vendor before/after marketing. This is the exception, and it should be the anchor of any care-side conversation:

Barrett MA, Allen A, Vuong VT, Zhu D, Rainey AJ, McConnell WM, Kho AN, Salisbury A, French DD. "Impact of an Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning Enhanced Electronic Health Record System on Quality Measures in Nursing Homes: A Difference-in-Differences Analysis." *Journal of the American Medical Directors Association*, July 2025, Vol 26 Issue 7. DOI 10.1016/j.jamda.2025.105680.

Verbatim: "218 nursing homes, with 94 using EHR+AI and 124 using EHR only"; "Eighteen quality measures from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) were analyzed over 6 quarters before and 5 quarters after"; the system "analyzed 150 daily clinical data elements per patient, alerting staff to changes in conditions, acuity, fall risk, and medication monitoring"; "**Statistically greater improvements were observed in 16 of 18 quality measures (89%) in EHR+AI sites**"; "greater reductions in major falls (**-9%, 95% CI -17, -1; P = .034**) and residents needing help with daily activities (**-22%, 95% CI -29, -15; P < .001**)". The authors themselves conclude "further research is needed". **[PUBLISHED]** — [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40383136](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40383136/).

Sourcing caveat: the abstract says "EHR+AI" without naming a vendor. Attribution to MatrixCare comes from trade press I could not open. Cite the study on its own terms.

**[INFERENCE]** Note the honest scale of the effect: −9% on major falls, from a controlled design. Set that against the 46–84% reductions vendors publish from within-cohort before/after comparisons. The gap between those two numbers is the credibility test for this entire category, and quoting the smaller, better-evidenced one is what will earn a clinician's trust.

### The statute and the codes

The governing instruments, all **[PUBLISHED]** via Retirement Commission Te Ara Ahunga Ora — [retirement.govt.nz/retirement-villages/the-act-regulations-and-codes](https://retirement.govt.nz/retirement-villages/the-act-regulations-and-codes/):

- Retirement Villages (General) Regulations 2006
- Retirement Villages (Dispute Panel) Regulations 2006
- Retirement Villages (Fees) Regulations 2006
- Retirement Villages (General) Amendment Regulations 2014
- Retirement Villages Code of Practice 2008 — amended October 2013 and April 2017
- Code of Residents' Rights — Schedule 4 of the Act

The Code of Practice "sets the minimum standards of how a village must operate" and covers required occupation right agreement terms and operator policies. The General Regulations "prescribe what must be included in key disclosure documents, occupation right agreements, registration requirements and deed of supervision arrangements with statutory supervisors." **[PUBLISHED]** — same source.

### The statutory protections a concept must not tread on

From the Summerset on the Landing disclosure statement **[PUBLISHED]**, which reproduces the Act's requirements:

- **15 working days to cancel.** "Under the Retirement Villages Act 2003, you are entitled to cancel your Occupation Right Agreement within 15 Working Days of signing it."
- **Independent legal advice is mandatory, not optional.** "You must obtain advice from a lawyer independent of the operator of the village before you sign an Occupation Right Agreement."
- **A statutory supervisor holds a first ranking mortgage** in residents' favour, and there is "a memorial on the title to the Land which protects your right to continue living in the Village no matter what happens to Summerset."
- **A four-step disputes path**: the operator's own complaints process, the statutory supervisor, independent mediation, then the disputes panel under the Act.

Any tool that gives a family a number, a comparison or a recommendation sits *upstream* of mandatory independent legal advice. It must hand off to that lawyer rather than substitute for them. This is not a nice-to-have — it is the design constraint that keeps the whole category safe.

### The regulator has already named the hard questions

This is the find I would build the entire pitch on. The Act's prescribed disclosure-statement wording tells intending residents, verbatim:

> "It is common for there to be misunderstandings by residents and their families about:
> - the kind of legal interest that the resident has in the village
> - what happens if the resident or their family wants to exit an Occupation Right Agreement
> - the fees and charges that apply to entering, moving between units within, and leaving the village
> - the ongoing fees and charges."

**[PUBLISHED]** — Disclosure Statement, Summerset on the Landing, 1 January 2026, Companies Office Retirement Villages Register.

The statute names the four things families misunderstand, and explicitly says *"and their families."* An operator that answers those four well is not doing marketing — it is doing what the Act says is commonly done badly. That is the framing for both pitches, and it is unarguable because it is the regulator's own language.

### The reform, and why it changes the brief

The Act is mid-reform. **[PUBLISHED]** — Ministry of Housing and Urban Development, page last updated 4 December 2025 — [hud.govt.nz/our-work/retirement-villages-act-2003](https://www.hud.govt.nz/our-work/retirement-villages-act-2003):

- **Repayments** — "A 12-month maximum repayment timeframe will be introduced", interest payable from six months after move-out, early access available for hardship or special needs, some exemptions for villages under 50 units.
- **Weekly fees and capital losses** — "Weekly fees will stop and fixed deductions will not accrue any higher when a resident moves out" and "Residents will not be liable for capital losses unless they benefit from capital gains."
- **Chattels and fixtures** — operators responsible for items they own and their maintenance; inventory lists required.
- **Disputes** — "A new, independent disputes scheme that's efficient and easy for residents to use will be established", operator-funded.
- **Disclosure and marketing** — simplified disclosure statements and ORAs; "Disclosure rules and obligations will be strengthened"; and the one that bites: **"The Registrar of Retirement villages will have new powers related to advertising material and registered documents that could mislead or deceive people."**

Timeline: an amendment bill "is expected to be introduced in the House by mid-2026" **[PUBLISHED]** — [hud.govt.nz/news/significant-changes-to-the-retirement-village-sector](https://www.hud.govt.nz/news/significant-changes-to-the-retirement-village-sector), announced 4 December 2025. Earlier, the three named review priorities were "Maintenance and repairs of operator-owned chattels and fixtures", "Managing complaints and disputes", and "Options for incentivising or requiring earlier capital repayments when residents move out of a village" **[PUBLISHED]** — Hon Tama Potaka, Associate Minister of Housing, 16 October 2024 — [beehive.govt.nz/release/priorities-set-retirement-villages-act-review](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/priorities-set-retirement-villages-act-review).

The Retirement Commissioner, Jane Wrightson, welcomed the package on 4 December 2025, noting reforms follow five years of review and "11,000+ submissions" on the 2023 discussion paper, and that "Operators must publish current disclosure statements online and strengthen obligations to ensure information is not misleading or deceptive." **[PUBLISHED]** — [retirement.govt.nz/news/latest-news/retirement-commission-welcomes-reform-of-retirement-villages-act](https://retirement.govt.nz/news/latest-news/retirement-commission-welcomes-reform-of-retirement-villages-act).

Sector scale, for context: "Over 53,000 people currently reside in retirement villages, with projections of nearly 113,000 by 2048" **[PUBLISHED]** — Beehive release, 16 October 2024, as above.

**Two consequences for every concept in this file.**

First, **an AI that generates a personalised cost estimate is advertising material.** Once the Registrar has powers over "advertising material … that could mislead or deceive", a generated number is not a neutral calculation — it is a representation by the operator. Any concept must therefore compute only from the operator's own registered documents, cite the clause, show the assumptions, and refuse to answer where the document is silent. Generative freehand pricing is off the table.

Second, **"operators must publish current disclosure statements online"** turns the reform into the business case. The obligation is coming anyway. A concept that makes the disclosure statement genuinely legible is compliance work that happens to be excellent marketing, which is a much better funding story than "a new AI channel".

### Privacy — and the part everyone will miss

- **Privacy Act 2020** and the 13 information privacy principles govern all of this. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner published AI guidance in September 2023 (preliminary guidance June 2023), framed around four questions: "Is the training data behind an AI tool relevant, reliable, and ethical?", "What was the purpose for collecting personal information? is your use related?", "How are you testing that AI tools are accurate and fair for your intended purpose?", and "What are you doing to track and manage new risks to information from AI tools?" The guidance cautions: "If in doubt, we recommend you do not use AI tools to handle personal information." **[PUBLISHED]** — [privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/ai](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/a-z-topics/ai/).
- **IPP3A came into force in May 2026.** It requires an agency collecting personal information about someone *from a source other than that person* to take reasonable steps to make that person aware of the collection, its purpose, the intended recipients, the collecting and holding agencies, and their access and correction rights — "as soon as is reasonably practicable after the information has been collected." **[PUBLISHED]** — [privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/privacy-principles/3a](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/privacy-principles/3a/).

  **This is the sharpest constraint in the whole category and it is four months old.** The core scenario for every concept here — an adult child typing their parent's age, health status, mobility and finances into an operator's tool — *is* indirect collection of the parent's personal information. IPP3A means the parent must be told. Every concept below therefore has to answer "what does Mum get told, and when?" A concept that quietly builds a profile of a parent from a child's answers is now not merely distasteful; it is likely non-compliant. Designed well, the notification is the feature — it is what makes the tool trustworthy rather than covert.
- **Codes of practice in force** under the Privacy Act 2020 include the **Health Information Privacy Code 2020** and the **Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025**; the codes "were updated early in 2026 to incorporate Information Privacy Principle 3A." **[PUBLISHED]** — [privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/codes-of-practice](https://www.privacy.org.nz/privacy-act-2020/codes-of-practice/).

  Consequence: care needs are health information under the HIPC. And any concept touching cameras, voice identification or gait analysis engages the Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025 — which rules out casually adding fall-detection vision to a family-facing product.

### AI policy settings in New Zealand

New Zealand's Strategy for Artificial Intelligence: Investing with Confidence was published July 2025 by MBIE. It is adoption-focused and does not create an AI-specific regulatory regime; its "Responsible AI guidance for businesses" is described as a voluntary resource. **[PUBLISHED]** — [mbie.govt.nz/…/new-zealands-ai-strategy-investing-with-confidence](https://www.mbie.govt.nz/business-and-employment/economic-growth/digital-policy/new-zealands-ai-strategy-investing-with-confidence); strategy PDF at [beehive.govt.nz](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2025-07/New%20Zealand's%20AI%20Strategy%20-%20Investing%20with%20confidence.pdf).

**[INFERENCE]** No AI-specific statute means the binding constraints on these concepts are the *sector* ones: the Retirement Villages Act and the incoming Registrar advertising powers, the Fair Trading Act 1986 on misleading conduct, the Privacy Act with IPP3A and the HIPC, and — care-side — the Ngā Paerewa Health and Disability Services Standard. Pitch the sector regime, not "AI regulation". A CTO in this sector is not worried about an AI Act; they are worried about the Registrar and the auditor.

**Two days before this file was compiled, Health New Zealand published an AI evaluation framework.** On 28 July 2026 Health NZ announced the **New Zealand AI Pre-Implementation Evaluation Framework**, for assessing whether AI tools are "safe, secure, ethical and equitable for patients, whānau and communities". Dr Rosie Dobson: "The framework is unique to the Aotearoa New Zealand context." **[PUBLISHED]** — [healthnz.govt.nz](https://www.healthnz.govt.nz/news-and-updates/consistent-approach-to-ai-tools-backed-by-new-national-framework). Note `tewhatuora.govt.nz` now redirects to `healthnz.govt.nz`.

**[INFERENCE]** The article makes no reference to aged residential care, so it does not obviously bind a retirement village operator. But it is two days old, it is the New Zealand health system's stated method for evaluating AI, and it names whānau explicitly. Referencing it — and offering to evaluate any concept against it voluntarily — is the single cheapest credibility move available in either pitch, and it will be new information to most people in the room.

### The best available governance reference is British

If either operator asks "what does good look like", the clearest published answer is the Care Quality Commission's, page last updated 21 May 2026. It sets out eleven principles providers "must follow". The first, verbatim, is the one to put on a slide:

> "AI to support, not to replace: AI can enhance, but not replace human decision making."

The rest cover human oversight, transparency and choice, safety and reliability, security, fairness and impartiality, AI readiness and training, effective governance, data protection impact assessment, accountability and procurement. Two further positions are worth quoting: "We do not assess or approve specific technologies" and "the absence or presence of AI does not predict a specific rating". Listed risks include that AI "can hallucinate and present untrue information, authoritatively as fact". **[PUBLISHED]** — [cqc.org.uk](https://www.cqc.org.uk/about-us/transparency/artificial-intelligence-health-social-care-cqcs-role-expectations-plans).

**[INFERENCE]** Worth naming out loud in either pitch: across the UK, Australia and New Zealand, **the regulators have published more specific thinking about AI than the operators they regulate.** The CQC's eleven principles, the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission's own AI Transparency Statement and Health NZ's framework are all more detailed than anything Summerset, Ryman, Anchor or Aveo has published. That is an unusual and useful inversion: aligning to the regulator's language is the fastest route to internal approval.

### Australia, briefly — because both operators have Australian villages

Summerset and Ryman both operate in Australia, so both regimes apply.

- **The Aged Care Act 2024 commenced 1 November 2025** **[PUBLISHED]** — [Department of Health, Disability and Ageing](https://www.health.gov.au/our-work/aged-care-act); [Federal Register of Legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00104/latest/text). A full-text grep of the compilation as at 1 July 2026 (891,926 characters) returns **zero** occurrences of "artificial intelligence" and **zero** of "algorithm". "Technology" appears 53 times, almost entirely as "assistive technology" in the subsidy provisions.
- **But the Act creates a statutory category that matters to anyone building a family-facing intermediary.** Chapter 3 is titled "Registered providers, aged care workers and **aged care digital platform operators**", and Part 6 defines them. Section 187 covers "an online enabled application, website or system operated to facilitate the delivery of services in the Commonwealth aged care system" where the operator "acts as an intermediary" and processes payments referable to that function. Section 188 imposes a duty to check and display whether a listed entity is a registered provider, with a **"Civil penalty: 500 penalty units"**. Section 189 adds notification, complaints and incident-management obligations and a requirement to "display on the platform, in a way that is prominent to individuals using the platform to access funded aged care services, the Statement of Rights", with a **"Civil penalty: 250 penalty units"**. **[PUBLISHED]** — same legislation URL.
  **[INFERENCE]** None of the eight concepts in this file is an intermediary marketplace — each is an operator's own tool about its own villages, which is outside s187. That is worth saying explicitly, because it is the difference between a product and a regulated platform. It is also a reason not to drift toward a comparison product later.
- **The ACCC's position on retirement villages is directly on point.** Operators should "give clear, honest and complete information when advertising, and in dealings with residents and prospective residents" and "give residents and prospective residents clear information about the purchase or payment model, and all applicable and optional fees and charges", and — plainly — "**Retirement village operators must not mislead consumers about fees and charges.**" **[PUBLISHED]** — [accc.gov.au](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/specific-products-and-activities/retirement-villages). Its guidance on home care also warns providers to "give customers time to seek advice and make decisions" and to "avoid adopting commission-based business models which might lead to pressure selling" **[PUBLISHED]** — [accc.gov.au](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/specific-products-and-activities/home-care-services).
- **"Consumers experiencing vulnerability or disadvantage" is an *enduring* ACCC enforcement priority** — "The ACCC therefore prioritises conduct that impacts these consumers." Notably, "artificial intelligence" is **not** a named 2026-27 priority in the ACCC's own words. **[PUBLISHED]** — [accc.gov.au](https://www.accc.gov.au/about-us/accc-strategy-and-priorities/compliance-and-enforcement-priorities).
- **Treasury has already tested AI against Australian consumer law and found it adequate** — Review of Artificial Intelligence and the Australian Consumer Law, final report 3 October 2025 **[PUBLISHED]** — [treasury.gov.au](https://treasury.gov.au/publication/p2025-702329). The line to know, verbatim from the Minister's release: "**As new technologies such as agentic AI develop, the Government will continue to review the Australian Consumer Law to ensure it is fit-for-purpose.**" **[PUBLISHED]** — [ministers.treasury.gov.au](https://ministers.treasury.gov.au/ministers/andrew-leigh-2025/media-releases/review-confirms-consumer-law-protects-australians-age).
- **Australia did not proceed with mandatory AI guardrails.** The National AI Plan, launched 2 December 2025: "The government's regulatory approach to AI will continue to build on Australia's robust existing legal and regulatory frameworks… Agencies and regulators will retain responsibility for identifying, assessing, and addressing potential AI-related harms within their respective policy and regulatory domains." An AI Safety Institute is being established. **[PUBLISHED]** — [industry.gov.au](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/national-ai-plan). The 2019 AI Ethics Principles have effectively been superseded by the October 2025 Guidance for AI Adoption; their Fairness principle names "vulnerable and underrepresented groups including, but not limited to, groups relating to age" **[PUBLISHED]** — [industry.gov.au](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/australias-ai-ethics-principles).
- **The Australian aged care regulator publishes its own AI Transparency Statement**, last updated 16 July 2026: "The Commission's AI Policy was approved on 10 December 2025"; "Our AI tools are not approved to make decisions or take administrative action without human intervention"; "The public does not currently directly interact with Commission AI systems when dealing with the Commission." **[PUBLISHED]** — [agedcarequality.gov.au](https://www.agedcarequality.gov.au/about-us/corporate-documents/ai-transparency-statement).
- **The OAIC has the most substantive published guidance touching vulnerable people's data in AI**, 21 October 2024: "the use of personal information in AI systems is a source of significant community concern and depending on the use case, may be a high privacy risk activity… the OAIC… expects organisations seeking to use AI to take a cautious approach." Also: "AI products should not be used simply because they are available"; due diligence "should not amount to a 'set and forget' approach"; and "ensuring that any public facing AI tools (such as chatbots) are clearly identified as such to users." **[PUBLISHED]** — [oaic.gov.au](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products).

**No Australian or New Zealand regulator has published anything specifically on AI and older consumers as a distinct cohort.** The closest hooks are the AI Ethics Principles' fairness reference to age, the ACCC's enduring vulnerability priority, and the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission's commitment to identifying negative impacts of AI use "on older people, aged care service recipients, representatives". **[PUBLISHED]** as cited. **[INFERENCE]** That absence is a risk, not a licence. It means the first operator to do this badly will define the category, and probably prompt the guidance.

### Sensitivity: what must not be built

Stated plainly, because several otherwise attractive mechanics fail here.

- **No urgency, scarcity or countdowns.** "3 villas left at this price" against a family deciding about a parent's decline is indefensible, and post-reform it is a Registrar problem as well as an ethical one.
- **No gamification.** No streaks, badges, progress bars or scores on a decision about a parent's health or a family's grief.
- **No inferred health status presented as fact.** A model guessing "your mother is likely to need hospital-level care within 18 months" is clinical prediction dressed as marketing. It engages the HIPC, it may be wrong, and it is cruel if it is right.
- **No profiling a parent behind their back.** IPP3A, and basic decency.
- **No AI-voiced calls to an older person.** Synthetic voice to over-75s who did not ask for it is straightforwardly wrong, whatever the consent position.
- **No lead scoring that changes what a family is told.** Ranking families by likelihood to buy and varying the information given is exactly what the incoming misleading-conduct powers exist to catch.
- **Bereavement is not a campaign trigger.** If a resident dies, every automated sequence stops. Full stop.

**[INFERENCE]** Ryman promises "clarity around the costs" and then makes the visitor wait for an email — while its own site does not publish the DMF accrual period. The gap between the promise and the wait is Ryman's specific opportunity, and it is a different gap from Summerset's.

## Four concepts for Summerset

Summerset's specific gap, in one line: **every number is deferred to a couriered pack, while every number is already public in a statutory PDF nobody reads.** All four concepts work that seam. Brand line to stay inside: "Love the life you choose" — the concepts should widen choice, never narrow it.

A rule that applies to all four: **they compute only from Summerset's own registered documents and quote the clause.** No generated pricing, no estimates, no "typically". Once the Registrar has powers over misleading advertising material, that is the only safe architecture — and it is also the only honest one.

### 1. While the Pack's in the Post

**Waiting moment** — the days between requesting the free information pack and the courier arriving. Summerset's own site sends every number into that pack, so the wait is currently dead air.

**What the family sees** — one page, sent to whoever requested the pack, for the specific village they asked about. It answers the four things the Act says residents and their families commonly misunderstand: the legal interest they'd hold, what happens on exit, the fees for entering, moving within, and leaving, and the ongoing fees. Every figure is drawn from that village's registered disclosure statement with the clause quoted and linked. Where the document is silent it says so and offers a human.

**Hard question answered** — "what does it really cost, and what do we get back?"

**Funded by** — sales and marketing, on pack-to-visit conversion. Co-owned with legal and compliance, because the incoming obligation to "publish current disclosure statements online" makes this a compliance asset that happens to be good marketing.

**Measured on** — share of pack recipients who arrive at a first visit already understanding the DMF and repayment mechanics; fall in late-stage "I didn't realise" objections logged by sales; and **families who self-select out early**, counted as a success, not a leak.

**Nearest real precedent** — Roobrik Surveys and Roobrik Afford: decision support placed deliberately before sales contact, for visitors "not ready for sales contact". Named customers Brightview Senior Living, Arete Living, Cedar Community; published claims "20-40% increase in quality web leads" and "up to 2x better SQL to Move-in rate vs other web leads". **[PUBLISHED]** — [roobrik.com](https://www.roobrik.com/), [roobrik.com/afford-senior-living-survey](https://roobrik.com/afford-senior-living-survey/). Note Roobrik's design choice worth copying: "no numbers required".

### 2. Same Page

**Waiting moment** — the weeks between a first village visit and a family decision, when siblings in three cities are forwarding each other PDFs and half-remembered phone calls.

**What the family sees** — one private space per enquiry. Each sibling asks in their own words; everyone gets the same answer, sourced to the same clause. A standing "what we don't know yet" list. A record of who has seen what, so no one can be accused of deciding without the others.

**Hard question answered** — "are we deciding this together, and are we all working from the same facts?" This is the sibling problem, and nothing in the NZ market addresses it.

**Funded by** — sales. Clearest commercial case of the four: stalled multi-decision-maker enquiries are the expensive ones.

**Measured on** — share of enquiries with more than one engaged family member; stall rate; volume of repeat explanations by sales staff. Explicitly **not** measured on time-to-close, so the tool is never tuned to hurry a family.

**Nearest real precedent** — **Birchgrove** in the UK, the only later-living operator anywhere I could find shipping an app explicitly for residents *and families*: "The Birchgrove App is invite-only and exclusively accessible to residents of Birchgrove neighbourhoods and their families" **[PUBLISHED]** — [birchgrove.life/app](https://www.birchgrove.life/app). It is a resident-services app rather than a decision workspace, so the sibling-coordination layer is genuinely unprecedented. Supporting evidence that the audience is real: Roobrik's published "70% family members/other" completion rate **[PUBLISHED]** — [roobrik.com](https://roobrik.com/senior-living-surveys/).

**Where this would be inappropriate** — never score or rank family members; never surface one sibling's private notes to another; never nudge toward a decision or flag "this family is cooling". The moment it recommends, it stops being a shared table and becomes a sales instrument.

### 3. When Things Change

**Waiting moment** — after a needs assessment, before a transfer. The most frightening and most opaque wait in the journey, and it happens to existing residents, so it is invisible to marketing.

**What the family sees** — plainly, what a move from villa to serviced apartment to care suite means at *their* village: DMF already accrued and how it carries across, the 25% overall cap, the 2% transfer administration fee and exactly when it is waived, the Dual Occupancy Fee if one of a couple moves, the daily care fee, and the Residential Care Subsidy and WINZ means-assessment path. Including the reassuring part Summerset already publishes but buries: "If your health needs necessitate a transfer into a Serviced Apartment or Care Suite we will not require you to make any additional upfront payment in order to make the transfer."

**Hard question answered** — "what happens when Mum's care needs change?"

**Funded by** — care operations, not sales. It serves people who have already bought. That is the honest funder and a stronger internal story than a lead metric.

**Measured on** — complaints and disputes about transfer costs; time from needs assessment to a settled family decision; family-reported clarity at the transfer conversation.

**Nearest real precedent** — Summerset's own *Understanding the cost for care* brochure (doc ref S0776/NOV 21), which already does this on paper for care rooms, including a published worked example. This concept is that brochure made live and village-specific. **[PUBLISHED]** — Summerset.

**Where this would be inappropriate** — **it must never predict decline.** It answers "if this happens, here is what it means" and never "your mother will likely need hospital-level care." It is triggered only by a needs assessment the family already holds, never by inference from behaviour, sensor data or staff notes. Predicting a parent's deterioration to their child is clinical prediction wearing a marketing badge, it engages the Health Information Privacy Code 2020, and it is cruel whether or not it is right.

### 4. Read It With Me

**Waiting moment** — the 15 working day statutory cancellation window, and the days waiting on a lawyer's appointment.

**What the family sees** — the occupation right agreement and disclosure statement, readable side by side, with any question answered against the clause. Its actual output is **a numbered list of questions to take to the independent lawyer**. It states on every screen that it is not legal advice and that independent legal advice before signing is mandatory under the Act.

**Hard question answered** — "what am I actually signing, and what should I be asking?"

**Funded by** — legal and compliance with sales. The strongest compliance narrative of the four: it improves the mandatory independent-advice step instead of competing with it.

**Measured on** — number and quality of questions families bring to their lawyer; solicitor feedback on the conversation; and cancellations inside the 15-day window — which may **rise**, and that must be accepted in advance as the tool working correctly.

**Nearest real precedent** — **ECH in South Australia**, which uses Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2 on AWS Bedrock to turn unstructured interview audio into structured care plans "aligned to ECH standards", moving creation "from 20 minutes to just 36 seconds" with accuracy "from 35.3% to 92.5%" **[PUBLISHED]** — [cgi.com](https://www.cgi.com/au/en-au/case-study/health/improving-quality-aged-care-generative-ai). Different document, identical architecture: constrained generation grounded in a controlled corpus. And **Regis Healthcare**, whose AI recommendations are "grounded in Regis's internal clinical policies" and reviewed by a named human role **[PUBLISHED]** — [Regis FY25 Annual Report](https://www.regis.com.au/site/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Regis-Healthcare-Limited-FY25-Annual-Report.pdf). Both are proof the grounded-and-reviewed pattern is already accepted in Australasian aged care.

**Where this would be inappropriate** — it must refuse to give legal advice, must never call a clause "standard" or "nothing to worry about", and must never imply a lawyer is optional. If it cannot reliably refuse, it should not ship.

## Four concepts for Ryman

Ryman's gap is a different shape. They publish a promise — the guides "give clarity around the costs" — then email a PDF, and their own site does not publish the DMF accrual period. They have two resident cohorts on different fee terms. And they already build real clinical technology in-house, so anything family-facing must be complementary to myRyman Care, never a substitute. Brand line to stay inside: "It's all taken care of" — which means **the operator does the work, not the family.** Ryman's concepts should remove effort; Summerset's should remove opacity.

### 1. The Guide That Answers Back

**Waiting moment** — between submitting the form and the guides email arriving, and then the far longer wait after reading them, when the real questions start.

**What the family sees** — the guides arrive as they do now, plus a private link to ask questions about them. Answers are grounded in Ryman's published material and the specific village's registered disclosure statement. Where Ryman does not publish something — the DMF accrual period being the obvious one — it says exactly that and routes the question to a person, rather than guessing.

**Hard question answered** — "you promised clarity on costs: what is the 30% actually, over what period, and what comes back to us?"

**Funded by** — marketing, on guide-to-visit conversion.

**Measured on** — guide-to-visit rate; and the running list of "we don't publish this" questions, which becomes a prioritised backlog of what Ryman should publish. That second metric is the real product, and it is the one to show a CTO.

**Nearest real precedent** — Roobrik Chat, which pairs customer service with optional decision-support modules on senior living sites. **[PUBLISHED]** — [roobrik.com](https://www.roobrik.com/).

**Where this would be inappropriate** — the "we don't publish this" answer must stay honest. The instant it fills a gap with a plausible-sounding number, it becomes misleading advertising material under the incoming Registrar powers.

### 2. Which Terms Are Ours

**Waiting moment** — the anxious days after a resident or their family reads a news story about Ryman raising fees. This wait is created by press coverage, not by a funnel, which is why nobody owns it.

**What the family sees** — after identity confirmation, the terms that apply to *their* agreement in plain language: DMF percentage, whether their weekly fee is fixed or indexed, the repayment mechanics, and the interest entitlement after six months — each cited to their own document. Plus an unambiguous statement of what has not changed for them.

**Hard question answered** — "has our deal changed, and can they change it later?"

**Funded by** — resident relations and operations, with the CFO's interest, because it defuses a recurring complaints and reputation load.

**Measured on** — fee-related complaint and call volume in the fortnight after fee coverage; call handling time; resident-reported trust.

**Nearest real precedent** — none in senior living that I could source; the closest working analogue is "your policy as it stands today" self-service in banking and insurance. Flagged in *What I could not verify* rather than dressed up.

**Where this would be inappropriate** — no upsell, ever. And access by an adult child requires the resident's consent: IPP3A and the Health Information Privacy Code 2020 both apply. If the resident lacks capacity, it runs through the enduring power of attorney and is documented, never on assumed consent.

### 3. How Mum's Week Was

**Waiting moment** — the long silence between visits, when an adult child in another city has no idea how their parent actually is. Weeks long, repeated indefinitely, and currently filled by phone calls to a village receptionist.

**What the family sees** — with the resident's explicit, revocable consent, a short weekly note in plain language about non-clinical life: what they joined in, how meals went, who visited, how they have seemed. Written from records staff already keep. Never vital signs, never assessments, never a risk score. myRyman Care stays exactly where it is — clinical and staff-facing.

**Hard question answered** — "will Mum actually be happy there?" — asked before the decision, and "is she alright?" — asked forever afterwards.

**Funded by** — operations. It displaces a genuinely large volume of unscheduled calls to village staff, which is a measurable and honest saving rather than a marketing claim.

**Measured on** — unsolicited family calls to village reception; family-reported reassurance; and consent and opt-out rates, where **a high opt-out rate is treated as a design failure**, not as friction to be engineered away.

**Nearest real precedent** — and this one is uncomfortably close to home. **Bupa New Zealand already ships a family-facing portal, blua, "currently available by invitation only at three care home sites"** **[PUBLISHED]** — [bupa.co.nz/blua](https://www.bupa.co.nz/blua/). Metlifecare has "A new digital resident portal… piloted in mid-FY26" **[PUBLISHED]** — Annual Report 2025. Internationally, **Birchgrove**'s app covers residents and families **[PUBLISHED]** — [birchgrove.life/app](https://www.birchgrove.life/app), and Amazon's Alexa Smart Properties for Senior Living publishes a "50-75%" increase in Atria Engage Life event participation **[PUBLISHED]** — [developer.amazon.com](https://developer.amazon.com/en-US/alexa/alexasmartproperties/seniorliving).

**On the objection that older residents will not engage** — which someone in the room will raise — the ElliQ programme is the answer: 834 enrolled and "more than 3,500 older adults have applied", 41 interactions per day at an average age of 75, and 60% still using it after three months **[PUBLISHED]** — [aging.ny.gov](https://aging.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2026/02/nysofa-elliq-project-update-2026.pdf). The demand is not the constraint.

**And the boundary language to borrow, from a direct NZ competitor** — Bupa NZ publishes that its AI platform "supports clinical, operational, and care teams **without automating care itself**", alongside "Healthcare is human. It requires compassion, connection and professional judgement." **[PUBLISHED]** — [bupa.co.nz](https://www.bupa.co.nz/life-at-bupa/news/bupa-invests-in-ai-to-support-smarter-safer-aged-care/). That is the right register for this whole category, and a competitor has already set it.

**Where this would be inappropriate** — **this is the concept most likely to be built badly, and the one to walk away from if the conditions cannot be met.** Non-negotiables: the resident consents and can revoke instantly and alone; the resident sees exactly what their family sees, always; no clinical content; no inference about mood, loneliness or decline; no cameras, no gait analysis, no voice identification — all of which would engage the Biometric Processing Privacy Code 2025. It is a reassurance product or it is nothing. If it starts describing what a parent did rather than what was offered to them, it has become surveillance of an older person for the comfort of a younger one, and it should be stopped.

### 4. Before You Say Anything

**Waiting moment** — the months before any enquiry exists, while an adult child works up to raising the subject. The longest wait in the category and the only one no operator serves.

**What the family sees** — no form, no village, no contact required. A private resource that helps someone prepare for a conversation with a parent who does not want to move, grounded in independent published guidance rather than Ryman's sales material. It does not recommend Ryman. Its whole job is to be the most useful thing available on the day someone searches that question at 11pm.

**Hard question answered** — "how do I raise this with Mum, who does not want to move?" The hardest question in the set, and in New Zealand it is close to unserved: Age Concern NZ publishes on housing options — "Other home options vs staying at home", "How to access home support services", "Staying in your home", "Advanced care planning: Your choice matters" — but nothing addressed to family members on raising relocation with a reluctant parent. **[PUBLISHED]** — [ageconcern.org.nz/information-advice/home-and-housing](https://ageconcern.org.nz/information-advice/home-and-housing/).

**Funded by** — brand, as brand investment, on a multi-year horizon. Be blunt with the CMO: weakest attribution of all eight, strongest brand return, and it only works if nobody attaches a lead target to it.

**Measured on** — brand consideration and assisted recall among 45–65s; organic share of voice on those queries. Deliberately **not** measured on lead capture.

**Nearest real precedent** — the family-conversation guidance published by ageing and dementia charities; see *The questions nobody answers well* for what exists and how good it is.

**Where this would be inappropriate** — this is where pressure would do the most harm and be most tempting. No lead capture, no retargeting pixel, no "families like yours chose Ryman", no follow-up email. **If Ryman cannot accept zero attribution, this concept should not be built** — a version of it with a lead form attached is worse than not doing it, because it monetises the moment a family is most frightened.

## The three findings I would lead with

1. **The answers families want are already published — as statutory PDFs on a Companies Office register, and as investor documents.** Summerset's weekly-fee-to-NZ-Super link, its full DMF accrual schedule, the 2% transfer fee, the Dual Occupancy Fee, its 91%/89% resident satisfaction scores and its expected tenure by unit type are all public. None of it is on a page a family will ever find. Nothing has to be invented to fix this, which is what makes it safe in a category where invention is dangerous.

2. **The regulator has already written the brief.** The Retirement Villages Act's prescribed disclosure wording states that "It is common for there to be misunderstandings by residents **and their families**" about the legal interest, the exit, the fees for entering and moving and leaving, and the ongoing fees. An operator that answers those four well is doing what the statute says is commonly done badly. And the incoming reform gives the Registrar "new powers related to advertising material… that could mislead or deceive people" — which makes grounded, clause-cited answers the only defensible architecture and turns the work into compliance rather than marketing.

3. **The category is empty, but not for much longer.** "Agentic" and "retirement village" have almost no shipped intersection anywhere in the world: the only product marketed as "fully agentic AI" in senior living is pre-launch with six design partners, and only 10% of surveyed operators use AI in operations at all. Meanwhile in New Zealand, Bupa has shipped care AI plus an invitation-only family portal, and Metlifecare has board-level AI governance and a resident portal piloting mid-FY26. Nobody has aimed any of it at the buying family — and both Summerset and Ryman are better placed to than they realise, because Summerset already runs a retrieval assistant over resident records internally and Ryman has publicly said it needs "additional director with customer and technology experience".
